# Voice-over script (record this first)

Read straight down. Leave about **one second of silence between lines** — it gives you cut points when you lay the picture over the audio.
Total speaking time is about **3 minutes 55 seconds**; with the gaps the finished video lands near 4:40, inside the 3–5 minute limit.

The timestamps are where each line starts if you read at a steady pace. They are a guide for lining up the picture, not a target to hit exactly.

Say "simulated" where it appears. Say "proposed extension", never "official extension". Never say the map score predicts pollution.

---


## SCENE 1 - The problem

**0:00** — These are ninety-six urban streams that the OneAquaHealth project monitors across five European cities.

**0:07** — Each of them was tested by a lab once. For ninety-five of them, that was back in 2023.

**0:15** — Between those campaigns, nobody knows what is in the water. And the things that harm health — a sewage misconnection, a spill — happen in between.


## SCENE 2 - The idea

**0:26** — Volunteers walk past these streams every day. Their reports live in a separate app: no health system can read them, nobody knows how far to trust them, and nothing follows from them.

**0:40** — StreamLink gives every stream one shared record instead. Here are five volunteer checks.


## SCENE 3 - Being a volunteer

**0:47** — A check takes three minutes and needs no expertise. The questions are OneAquaHealth's own, turned into pictures.

**0:55** — Shape of the channel, the bed, the banks. Anything you are unsure about, you just say so.

**1:03** — And this is what maps and yearly lab visits miss: a pipe discharging today.

**1:10** — It even asks how the place makes you feel, because the project links stream health to human wellbeing.

**1:18** — Say the volunteer sums it up as good overall — that is the mistake we want to catch.

**1:27** — It works with no signal, too. The check waits on your phone and sends itself later.


## SCENE 4 - Trust

**1:35** — Before anything is sent, the app checks the answers against each other. Here the volunteer rated the stream as good, but also reported sewage.

**1:45** — The volunteer fixes it, and the trust score goes up.

**1:51** — The second flag is only a reminder that a photo helps; the volunteer confirms there isn't one.

**1:59** — Every flag, and how it was resolved, is stored with the record, so an expert can see why it deserves trust.

**2:09** — Now it is saved — as preliminary data.


## SCENE 5 - What the city does

**2:13** — This is the stream's record: the stream and its banks, animals and disease vectors, and people's health — each showing how old that part of the picture is.

**2:26** — An expert verifies it. Only then does it become final and conform to OneAquaHealth's official indicator profile — so unverified crowd data can never pretend to be project data.

**2:38** — The city requests a lab visit, and the request carries its reasons: a fresh verified report, a lab picture that is two years old, and the map context.

**2:50** — The lab reports back, and that closes the request.

**2:55** — And the volunteer is told what their report led to. That is the part that keeps people checking.


## SCENE 6 - The proof

**3:04** — None of this sits in a private database. Everything you just saw is standard health data.

**3:11** — And this is the same record, read back from the organizers' own FHIR server.

**3:18** — Here it is on their server, claiming two profiles: ours, and OneAquaHealth's official indicator profile.

**3:26** — Every record we write is checked by the official HL7 validator: ninety-one files, zero errors — plus five broken records it correctly rejects.


## SCENE 7 - Anywhere, and honesty

**3:36** — Adding a city is one config file. Singapore has no OneAquaHealth lab data at all, so the map baseline carries it — and the app says so plainly.

**3:48** — We tested that map score by holding out whole cities. One signal survived: within a city, streams closer to a wastewater plant rank higher for lab risk.

**4:00** — Comparing cities did not hold up, so we do not claim it. The richer model we tried first did worse than chance, and we say that too.

**4:12** — It is open source, it says exactly what is real and what is simulated, and the extension we wrote is ready to hand back to the project.


*(ends about 4:24 including the one-second gaps)*
